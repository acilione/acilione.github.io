               The Incredible Amount of Physics Behind the Heron’s Fountain
                                                            (Dated:)
               Heron’s fountain looks like a simple trick: water spurts upward as if the device could run forever.
             In reality, it is a showcase of physics at work—gravity, air compression, viscosity, turbulence, and
             energy dissipation all intertwined. Surprisingly, this classic demonstration has rarely been treated
             beyond a qualitative level. In this work we build a consistent theoretical framework, extending
             Bernoulli’s equation to include viscous effects and clarifying the limits of its applicability within the
             system. We complement the model with carefully designed experiments, achieving direct comparison
             between predictions and data. The result is a new perspective on a two-thousand-year-old invention,
             where a classroom curiosity becomes a precise tool to explore fundamental fluid physics.


                 I.   INTRODUCTION


  Heron’s fountain,1 invented by the Greek engineer
Heron of Alexandria in the 1st century AD, is a classic
demonstration of hydraulics and pneumatics. It oper-
ates using gravity and air pressure—without any exter-
nal pump—to make water spout from a basin. Figure 1
shows a schematic representation of such a device. The
upper reservoir is open to the atmosphere and communi-
cates with the lower airtight chamber through two thin
vertical pipes, hereafter referred to as the left and right
pipes, respectively. The regions labeled A, B, and C con-
tain water, while region D contains air and is sealed from
the external environment. As a result, the air pressure
in region D generally differs from atmospheric pressure.
When the plug (the black triangle in the figure) seal-
ing the hole at the bottom of region A is removed, the
fountain begins to operate. The mechanism is as follows:
water flows from region A to region B through the left
pipe, increasing the pressure in region D until it becomes
sufficient to push water upward through the right pipe,
producing the upper jet, which falls back into region A.

ously refilled by the fountain’s own jet, the system is
